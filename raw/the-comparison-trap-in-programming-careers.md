## Two Forms of Comparison

### Form 1: The LinkedIn Stage vs. Your Backstage

The comparison hit me in two different ways early in my career.

The first was seeing people who started at the same time as me already showing off high salaries, landing top roles at big companies, building massive projects, and posting about it on LinkedIn — while I sat there staring at my code, not feeling ready to even apply for an internship.

The desperation was real.

But it took me a while to understand the obvious: **you don't live 24 hours a day with those people.** You don't see their all-nighters, what they sacrificed, or the background they already had coming in. I was making the classic mistake of comparing my backstage to everyone else's stage. I was measuring my first step with the yardstick of someone already halfway up the staircase.

### Form 2: Capacity vs. Familiarity

The second form of comparison gave me a sense of inadequacy — a feeling of being slow or dumb.

I was confusing **capacity** with **familiarity**.

When I entered tech, I felt like I was starting late. I saw code for the first time at 17, and I'd look around and it seemed like my peers were absorbing everything by osmosis. They'd master in a week what took me a month or more — and I'd think: *I must be incapable.*

But here's the truth: most of the time, what you're watching is a **lifetime of accumulated familiarity with technology**, not raw talent. Someone who grew up in front of a computer naturally accelerates much faster at the beginning. Imagine someone who had robotics classes, programming in school, or spent years actually building things with technology — of course they have an easier time.

That doesn't mean we lack talent. It just means **our starting line was in a completely different place**, and we'll need to work a bit harder to close that gap and build the fluency others already brought with them.

## The Only Comparison That Makes Sense

In the end, the only comparison that truly matters is **who I am today versus who I was yesterday**. Everything else is noise.

## Strategies for Tracking Personal Progress

### 1. Revisit Old Code

I'd open code I wrote three months ago — especially a project I finished feeling proud of, thinking it was polished and great. When I came back to it later and thought *"What ugly code — I'd do this so much better now"* — that was my proof of growth.

That small sting of shame at old code was the most concrete evidence that my technical level was rising and my career was moving forward.

### 2. Redo Exercises from Scratch

This was one of the things that gave me the most confidence.

I'd take a challenge that took me a week of struggle to solve and try to solve it again from scratch — without looking at my old solution. I'd notice I was much faster, that I already had the pattern in my head, and that what used to take me hours or days to reason through, I was now coding almost automatically.

I already knew the way.

### 3. Keep a Learning Log

I kept a simple log — at least one new thing I'd learned each week or each day.

At the end of the month, I'd look back at the list of technical terms, libraries, and technologies, and realize that even in months where I felt like I was going nowhere, **I had actually accumulated a lot**.

### 4. Celebrate When Bugs Stopped Bullying Me

I started paying attention to how much less time I needed to solve problems that used to paralyze me.

A great example: the semicolon. The first time it breaks your code, you're lost — you have to look it up, figure out what's missing. Over time, it becomes automatic: *"Oh, just a missing semicolon."* 

That started happening with every error. Bugs that once sent me into despair for days were getting resolved instantly. That was a clear signal I was building repertoire.
